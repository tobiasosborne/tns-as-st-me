Prologue · before any of the new work

The infrared triangle in its home setting

Three subjects that grew up apart — soft theorems, asymptotic symmetries, and the memory effect — turn out to be one subject. This page is an attempt to show you why, in the setting where the statement was first made: ordinary four‑dimensional spacetime, with charges and gravitational waves.

It is written for a physicist who is comfortable with quantum mechanics, general relativity, QED‑level field theory and many‑body physics, and who has never had a reason to care about soft factors. Nothing here assumes you already know what a supertranslation is. Every station opens in plain language with something you already own — a Coulomb field, a wave packet, an interferometer — before anything new is asked of you.

The arc is short. Why should anyone expect a permanent trace to be left behind by radiation that has long since passed? Then the machine: two systems simple enough to solve honestly, a charge that gets kicked and a burst of null energy crossing the sky, worked out far enough that you can aim them yourself and read the numbers off. Then the triangle, assembled out of what the machine actually showed.

A note on ambition, so it is not oversold. This page is the top rung of a ladder. The triangle is stated here in the place it is best established: 3+1 dimensions, flat at infinity, classical radiation fields. The research this prologue introduces asks the same three questions of much smaller systems — a one‑dimensional lattice of spins, and a pair of thermal boundary clocks — where some of the answers are proved, some are computed, and some turn out to be no. Station 10 lays out those two lower rungs honestly, and that is where this page stops.

Ten stations. The firing range is station 5.

Station 1 · the question

Does anything stay different afterwards?

Two masses float freely in space, far from everything, a fixed distance apart. A gravitational wave passes. While it passes, their separation oscillates — this is what an interferometer is built to catch. Then the wave is gone.

Here is the question this whole page exists to answer. When the wave has passed and everything is quiet again, are the two masses back where they started?

Nothing in your training says they must be. Nothing obvious says they must not. The honest position, before doing any work, is that you do not know — and the answer turns out to matter far more than it looks like it should.

Both endings above are consistent with everything an undergraduate course tells you about gravitational waves. Deciding between them is not a matter of taste, and it is not a small technical footnote: the answer is yes, something stays different, the permanent offset is fixed by the energy that flew past, and following that fact carefully is what connects three fields that had no idea they were the same one.

So: a wave goes by, things wobble, the wobble stops. Whether anything remains different is the whole subject.

Station 5 · the firing range

Shoot the detectors

Everything up to here has been argument. This station is a range. There are detectors out there at various angles and distances; you aim, you fire, and you watch what they do — and then you read off, detector by detector, whether the thing they kept agrees with the formula that says what they should have kept.

There is no score. The point is the aiming and the watching. Every number in the ledger underneath is computed from the equations printed beside it, and the disagreement column is the honest residual of two genuinely different routes to the same quantity, not a decoration.

A single charge sits at rest. At some moment something kicks it, and it leaves with velocity βfc. The field lines cannot rearrange faster than light, so the news of the kick leaves as a spherical pulse. Out at the detectors, free test charges wait. Each one feels the pulse go by, jitters, and is left drifting — with a velocity it did not have before.

The field the detector actually sees — radiative part, exact
\[ \mathbf{E}_{\rm rad}(t)\;=\;\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 c R}\; \frac{\hat n\times\big[(\hat n-\boldsymbol\beta)\times\dot{\boldsymbol\beta}\big]} {\left(1-\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\beta\right)^{3}}\bigg|_{t'}, \qquad t \;=\; t'+\frac{R-\hat n\cdot\mathbf r_s(t')}{c} \]
This is the acceleration term of the Liénard–Wiechert field — the piece that survives as 1/R and carries the radiation. The Coulomb term falls as 1/R² and is not part of the radiative data; it is not included in the ledger, and the ledger says so. The retarded-time relation above is inverted exactly, by bisection.
What the whole pulse adds up to — closed form
\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathbf E_{\rm rad}\,dt \;=\; \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 c R}\left[\; \frac{\boldsymbol\beta_{i\perp}}{1-\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\beta_i} -\frac{\boldsymbol\beta_{f\perp}}{1-\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\beta_f}\;\right] \]
One line of algebra gets you there, and it is worth seeing, because the whole subject is hiding in it: \[ \frac{\hat n\times\big[(\hat n-\boldsymbol\beta)\times\dot{\boldsymbol\beta}\big]} {\left(1-\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\beta\right)^{2}} \;=\;-\,\frac{d}{dt'}\!\left[\frac{\boldsymbol\beta_{\perp}}{1-\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\beta}\right] , \qquad \frac{dt}{dt'}=1-\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\beta . \] The integrand is a total derivative in the source's own time. So the answer depends on the velocity before and the velocity after, and on nothing else about the trip in between. Try it: the overshoot and settle profile takes a wildly different path to the same final velocity, and the ledger does not move.

Now fire energy instead of a charge. A burst of massless stuff leaves the origin with an angular pattern you choose — one beam, or two, wide or tight. Out at the detectors sit rings of free masses. Each ring rings as the burst goes past it, and each ring is left a different shape than it started.

The matching parts of the flux are not free either — they are the change in the Bondi mass and momentum, the ΔmB in the quoted constraint. Energy and momentum conservation, and nothing else, live at ℓ ≤ 1.

The soft-photon ledger is the more startling of the two, because you can break it and it does not break. Change the pulse duration, change the path the charge takes to get up to speed, make it overshoot and come back — the transient that each detector feels changes completely, and the velocity it is left with does not move in the twelfth digit. That is not a coincidence to be explained later; it is station 6's identity, seen from the range.

The gravitational ledger has its own surprise, and it is geometric. Look at where the rings that remember most are sitting. They are not where you aimed.

Station 8 · the gravitational memory range, taken apart

Which angular structures survive into memory

You almost never solve a field equation on the sphere point by point. You expand in multipoles, because that turns the equation into one algebraic relation per multipole, and then you read the physics off the coefficients. The memory equation is exactly that kind of equation, and its coefficients are unusually blunt instruments.

Station 5 gave you the whole solve at once. This station opens it up. The constraint, written multipole by multipole, is a single division:

The memory equation, one line per multipole
\[ \Phi_{\ell m}\;=\;\frac{4\,F_{\ell m}}{(\ell-1)\ell(\ell+1)(\ell+2)},\qquad \ell\ge 2 \]
The denominator grows like ℓ4. Any fine angular structure in the energy flux is divided by a large number before it reaches the memory. The memory is a heavily low-passed image of the flux — that is the whole content of this station, and everything below is you checking it.

That has a consequence you can see rather than infer. The memory pattern on the sky does not look like the burst. It is smeared over the whole sphere, including places the burst never went — and it has zeroes in places you would have bet against.

“One of its unusual characteristics is that it is highly nonlocal on S². Indeed, if a gravity wave passes through the north pole, the effect vanishes both there and at the south pole but is large near the equator.”

Quoted soft_Arxiv_update.tex:2641. The map above is that sentence, computed.

Station 9 · the hinge, and what it rests on

A pole at zero frequency is a step in time

Everything so far has hung on one sentence, usually said quickly: the Fourier transform of a pole is a step function, so a soft pole and a permanent shift are the same statement. It is true. It also has hypotheses, and they are the kind that quietly fail.

The familiar anchor is a driven damped oscillator. You hit it, it rings, the ringing dies, and it comes to rest. Where it comes to rest is the zero-frequency part of its response function — the DC response. Everything below is that, made exact and then broken on purpose.

The reason to labour this is that the residue never stops being the residue. Put the undamped pair back on the real axis and −iωf̃(ω) at ω → 0 is exactly what it always was. What is gone is the thing it was supposed to equal. The soft factor is a statement about a pole; the memory is a statement about a limit; they agree when, and only when, the rest of the response settles down.

In stations 5 and 8 the hypotheses held, and were checked. It is worth knowing that they were doing work.

Station 10 · the triangle, and where it goes next

Three subjects, one subject

Here is the picture the whole page was assembling. Three corners, three edges. Every one of them is something you have now watched happen, except one, which is reported rather than derived and is labelled as such.

Click a corner or an edge.

Now the honest part. Every corner of that triangle assumed something so quietly that it is easy to miss: detectors whose rulers do not change while they are measuring. The rings in station 5 are inertial, isolated, and cold. The charges are free. The whole notion of a permanent shift is a comparison of a ruler against itself, before and after, and it needs the ruler to have been the same object both times.

That assumption gets harder to make as you go down in dimension and up in temperature. Which raises the question this campaign is actually about: what survives when the ruler is part of a thermal state?

A pair of black-hole boundary clocks

Two boundaries of a nearly-AdS2 throat, in a thermal state — the setting where the ruler really is thermal, because the clock and the thing it measures are the same degree of freedom. Here the answers split. A calibrated relational susceptibility does reproduce the DC soft residue at order 1/C, and the numerics agree with the closed form to 0.011 per cent. An absolute late-time plateau is simply not there. And the butterfly shift, the obvious candidate for a memory in this system, has no plateau at all: it is a transient time delay, and calling it memory would be wrong.

Those are the claims as they stand in the repository, with their scopes. Two of them are refusals. That is what a ladder looks like when it is being climbed honestly rather than advertised.

End of the prologue

What this page did and did not do

It showed you two systems worked out far enough to aim and to check: a charge that gets kicked, and a burst of energy crossing the sky. In the first, the permanent velocity kick a distant test charge is left with was computed twice — once from the endpoints alone, once by integrating the entire field history — and the two agree to the last digit that double precision has. In the second, the permanent strain was solved multipole by multipole from a constraint quoted out of the lecture notes, and the solve was checked against the Green’s function those same notes give, to twelve digits.

It did not derive the Ward identity connecting the symmetry corner to the soft corner. That result is reported here, with its source line, and nothing on this page should be read as having established it.

What it was for: to make the infrared triangle something you have handled, before being asked whether the same three questions have answers in systems where the detector is not an idealisation. That is the next rung down, and the one after, and they are where the work is.

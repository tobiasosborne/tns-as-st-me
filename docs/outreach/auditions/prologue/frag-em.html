Station 2 · the bedrock

A charge is kicked, and the news travels outward in a shell

Start with something you already own. A charge sitting still has a field that points straight out from it and falls off as one over distance squared; its field lines are radial spokes that go on forever. A charge moving at a constant velocity has the same spokes, drawn from wherever the charge is now, but squashed towards the plane at right angles to the motion.

That second fact is worth writing down, because everything on this page is built out of it. Heaviside’s form of the field of a uniformly moving charge is exact and elementary — it is a boosted Coulomb field, nothing more:

The field of a charge in uniform motion — exact, and radial from its present position
\[ \vec E(\vec x,t)\;=\;\frac{q\,(1-\beta^{2})\,\vec R}{\bigl(R_{\parallel}^{2}+(1-\beta^{2})\,R_{\perp}^{2}\bigr)^{3/2}}\,, \qquad \vec R=\vec x-\vec x_{\text{now}}(t) \]
Here \(R_\parallel\) and \(R_\perp\) are the parts of \(\vec R\) along and across the velocity. The field points exactly along \(\vec R\), so the field lines are straight rays from the charge’s present position — not from where it was when the field left it. At \(\beta=0\) this is Coulomb’s law. As \(\beta\to1\) the denominator collapses except near \(R_\parallel=0\), which is the squashing.

Now the only new thing in this station. Take a charge that has been drifting at one velocity forever, give it a sharp kick lasting a time \(\tau\), and let it drift at a new velocity forever after. Both halves of the answer are the formula above. The question is what happens in between.

Causality answers it, and it is worth being exact about where the boundaries are. The kick occupies the source times \(-\tau/2\) to \(+\tau/2\), and the charge is moving throughout, so the news of its beginning and the news of its end leave from two different places. At page time \(t\) the two faces of the shell are therefore

Outside the outer face nothing has happened yet, so the field there is still the old one — drawn from where the charge would have been had it never been kicked. Inside the inner face the field is the new one, drawn from where the charge actually is. In between, the field lines have to get across, because field lines do not begin or end in empty space. They cross almost sideways. That sideways piece is the radiation, and this figure is the whole of it.

The two faces of the shell at page time \(t\) — exact, and not concentric
\[ \bigl|\vec x-\vec r(-\tau/2)\bigr|=c\left(t+\tfrac{\tau}{2}\right), \qquad \bigl|\vec x-\vec r(+\tau/2)\bigr|=c\left(t-\tfrac{\tau}{2}\right) \]
\(\vec r(\mp\tau/2)\) are the places the charge occupied when the kick began and when it ended, and it moved between them, so the two spheres have different centres. The radial distance between them, measured out from the origin, is therefore not \(c\tau\) and depends on the direction you look: the figure prints it live at the probe. \(c\tau\) is the duration of the kick in source time, which sets the scale of that width but equals it only in the one case where the charge did not move while it was being kicked. At the settings this figure opens with, the width runs from \(0.0653\) at \(0^{\circ}\) to \(0.1148\) at \(180^{\circ}\) while \(c\tau=0.090\).

The construction that decides which outer line joins which inner line is a counting argument, and it is worth a line of algebra because it is the only thing in the figure that is not just Heaviside’s formula. A tube of field lines carries a fixed amount of flux, and there is no charge in the shell for it to gain or lose any. So label each line by the flux it carries. In the charge’s own rest frame the lines are evenly spread, so the label is simply \(s=\cos\theta_{\text{rest}}\); in the lab, the fixed-time slice through that line is length‑contracted along the motion:

The lab direction of the field line labelled \(s\), for a charge of speed \(\beta\)
\[ \tan\theta_{\text{lab}}=\gamma\,\tan\theta_{\text{rest}} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \cos\theta_{\text{lab}}=\frac{s}{\gamma\sqrt{1-s^{2}\beta^{2}}}\,,\qquad \gamma=(1-\beta^{2})^{-1/2} \]
The matching rule is then as short as it could be: the same \(s\) on both sides of the shell. The check that this is the right map: integrating Heaviside’s field over a cone of half‑angle \(\theta\) about the velocity gives the enclosed flux \(2\pi q\bigl[1-\cos\theta/\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\bigr]\), and setting that equal on the two sides returns exactly the line above. An aside that is worth the thirty seconds, because the literature labels these lines the other way. The angle above is measured from the charge’s present position, and the map is length contraction. If you instead label a line by the direction from which it leaves the origin — where the kick happened — you get the relativistic aberration formula, which looks like a completely different answer and beams things forward rather than sideways. It is not a different answer. Follow the ray from the present position out until it crosses the sphere \(r=ct\) and read off its direction from the centre, and you land on the aberrated direction exactly; the reference core labels the lines that way and the two agree to nine digits at every angle we checked. Two names for one construction. The figure does not take even that on trust: it streamlines the exact field across the shell and prints how far the streamline lands from the prediction.

Push \(\tau\) down and watch the ratio in the readout climb. Nothing about the two straight regions changed; the shell just got thinner, so the same sideways offset had to be taken up over a shorter distance. In the limit the sideways field goes as \(1/r\) rather than \(1/r^{2}\), which is what makes it radiation: it survives to infinity, and the energy it carries does not fall off with distance. And the offset it has to take up is set by the change in velocity — by the kick, and by nothing else about how the kick was administered.

That last sentence is the reason this station exists. The information that the charge was kicked is carried outward in a shell, and what is written in the shell is a permanent fact about the difference between the old velocity and the new one. The rest of this page is mostly about learning to read it.

Station 3 · the same event, in frequency

A sharp kick has a flat spectrum, and that is a pole

Nothing new happens in this station. It is the same charge and the same kick as station 2, looked at through a Fourier transform instead of a clock.

The familiar anchor is any short pulse. Take a wave packet and squeeze it in time: its spectrum spreads out. In the limit of a very short pulse the spectrum is flat — the pulse contains every frequency in equal measure, up to a ceiling set by how short it actually was. That is what a sharp kick does to the radiation field, and everything below is that statement made exact.

The exact energy radiated per unit frequency per unit solid angle, for any trajectory, is a single integral over the charge’s own time. We use it as written:

Energy per unit frequency per unit solid angle, exact for any trajectory
\[ \frac{d^{2}I}{d\omega\,d\Omega} \;=\;\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\left|\;\int dt\; \frac{\hat n\times\bigl[(\hat n-\vec\beta)\times\dot{\vec\beta}\bigr]}{(1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta)^{2}}\; e^{\,i\omega\,(t-\hat n\cdot\vec r(t))}\right|^{2} \]
The integrand is nonzero only while the charge is being kicked, so the integral runs over one window of width \(\tau\). The page evaluates it by composite eight‑node Gauss–Legendre quadrature with the panel count rising in proportion to \(\omega\tau\), so the oscillating phase is always resolved. Units are Gaussian with \(c=\hbar=1\), and the charge is one elementary charge, \(q^{2}=\alpha\).

As \(\omega\to0\) the exponential goes to one and the integral can be done in closed form, because it is then a total derivative:

The \(\omega\to0\) limit — closed form, no quadrature
\[ \int dt\;\frac{\hat n\times\bigl[(\hat n-\vec\beta)\times\dot{\vec\beta}\bigr]}{(1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta)^{2}} \;=\;\hat n\times\Bigl(\hat n\times\Bigl[\tfrac{\vec\beta_{f}}{1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta_{f}}-\tfrac{\vec\beta_{i}}{1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta_{i}}\Bigr]\Bigr) \;\equiv\;\vec S(\hat n) \]
One line of algebra: the integrand is exactly \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\Bigl[\dfrac{\hat n\times(\hat n\times\vec\beta)}{1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta}\Bigr]\), so the integral only sees the endpoints. Nothing about the shape or the duration of the kick survives — only the two velocities. That independence is the whole reason the low‑frequency end of the spectrum is universal, and it is the same fact that made the sideways offset in station 2 depend only on the change in velocity.

So the classical calculation hands us a number, \(\vec S(\hat n)\), that controls the whole low‑frequency end of the radiation and depends on nothing but the momenta going in and out. A number of that description already exists in quantum field theory, and it is older. Here is Weinberg’s soft photon theorem as the lecture notes write it. The source lines are reproduced first, exactly as they stand in the TeX, and the same equation is set below them with the paper’s five abbreviations written out; nothing else is changed.

The relation to what we just computed is algebra, not analogy. Write the hard particle’s momentum as \(p^{\mu}=E(1,\vec\beta)\) and the soft photon’s as \(q^{\mu}=\omega(1,\hat n)\), and take the polarization purely spatial:

One term of Weinberg’s bracket, on this kinematics
\[ \frac{p\cdot\varepsilon}{p\cdot q}\;=\;\frac{1}{\omega}\,\frac{\vec\beta\cdot\vec\varepsilon}{1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta} \]
The energy \(E\) cancels between numerator and denominator, which is why the soft factor knows about directions and charges and nothing else. In the reference core’s own words, which are the sentence this page is licensed to print: “for \(q^{\mu}=\omega(1,\hat{\mathbf n})\), \(p^{\mu}=m\gamma(1,\boldsymbol\beta)\), and transverse polarization, \(p\cdot\epsilon/(p\cdot q)=\boldsymbol\beta\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon/ [\omega(1-\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta)]\); therefore the kicked charge’s classical \(1/\omega\) coefficient is exactly the single‑emitter kinematic factor in Weinberg’s leading soft theorem (out minus in), with the displayed charge and coupling.” One sign, and it is not free. Two different objects are in play and this page has already fixed both, so the relation between them is a fact rather than a convention. Call the current coefficient \(S_{\text{cl}}=\bigl[\tfrac{\vec\beta_f}{1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta_f}-\tfrac{\vec\beta_i}{1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta_i}\bigr]\cdot\vec\varepsilon\). The vector \(\vec S\) defined above is that same bracket with the double cross product in front of it, and for a transverse polarization \(\hat n\cdot\vec\varepsilon=0\), so \(\hat n\times(\hat n\times\vec A)\cdot\vec\varepsilon=-\vec A\cdot\vec\varepsilon\). Hence \(\vec S(\hat n)\cdot\vec\varepsilon=-S_{\text{cl}}\), and Weinberg’s bracket for our one incoming and one outgoing line, both of charge \(Q\), is \(\frac{Q}{\omega}S_{\text{cl}}=-\frac{Q}{\omega}\,\vec S(\hat n)\cdot\vec\varepsilon\). The magnitudes are the same, and \(|\vec S|^{2}\) — which is what the spectrum above and the sphere in station 4 are built from — does not see the sign at all. The reference core keeps the two apart in exactly this way: its softAmplitude returns \(S_{\text{cl}}\), its dcRadiationField returns the vector \(\vec S\), and its notes record \(\vec S\cdot\vec\varepsilon=-S_{\text{cl}}\). What this is not. It is not a derivation of the soft theorem, and it is not a claim that the classical calculation implies the quantum one. It is the observation that the two expressions are the same function of the same data, checked numerically below.

One consequence is worth stating plainly, because it is the thing that makes the deep infrared awkward rather than merely universal. The energy in the plateau is finite: \(\int d\omega\) of a constant, up to the cutoff, converges. The number of photons is \(\int d\omega/\omega\) of a constant, and that does not.

Read that table honestly. It does not say that infinitely many photons are produced by an experiment; it says that the question “how many photons” has no answer until you say how soft a photon you are prepared to count, and that the answer then grows without bound as you lower the threshold, logarithmically and therefore slowly. Every real detector has such a threshold, and every measured cross‑section is a statement about photons above it. The energy in those photons stays finite throughout, which is why the physics is not in trouble even though the counting is.

What the next stations do with \(\vec S(\hat n)\) is take it seriously as a function on the sphere of directions, and then ask what conserved quantity it is the flux of.

Station 4 · the soft factor on the celestial sphere

Every hard particle paints a ring on the sky, with an exact zero at its centre

The familiar anchor is a radiation pattern. A charge shaken gently along an axis — nonrelativistically, so that the usual dipole formula applies — radiates as \(\sin^{2}\theta\): a doughnut, with nothing along the axis. You have drawn that picture. Relativistic motion tilts the doughnut forward, because of the beaming denominator that appears below, but it does not fill in the hole. What follows is the same kind of picture for the softest photons of a scattering event, and it is one of the few things in field theory that can be drawn exactly.

Station 3 produced a vector \(\vec S(\hat n)\) for one charge changing velocity once. Nothing in that calculation cared that there was only one charge, or that it was a single kick. For any collection of charges that come in on straight lines and leave on straight lines, the same argument gives one term per line:

The soft factor for a scattering event, and the pattern it paints on the sphere
\[ \vec S(\hat n)=\hat n\times\Bigl(\hat n\times\sum_{k}\eta_k Q_k\frac{\vec\beta_k}{1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta_k}\Bigr), \qquad \mathcal{P}(\hat n)\;\equiv\;\bigl|\vec S(\hat n)\bigr|^{2} \]
\(\eta_k=+1\) for an outgoing line and \(-1\) for an incoming one; \(Q_k\) is its charge and \(\vec\beta_k\) its velocity. \(\mathcal{P}\) is what the sphere below is painted with. It is the polarization sum, in the sense that for any two orthonormal polarizations \(\vec\varepsilon_1,\vec\varepsilon_2\) perpendicular to \(\hat n\), \(|\vec S\cdot\vec\varepsilon_1|^{2}+|\vec S\cdot\vec\varepsilon_2|^{2}=\mathcal{P}(\hat n)\); the figure builds two such polarizations at the probe direction and prints the difference.

Two features are worth watching for before you touch anything, and the first one is a small surprise. Each line contributes a beaming denominator \(1/(1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta_k)\), which is largest when you look along that line’s own direction — but the double cross product in front of it removes exactly the piece that points along \(\hat n\). Look straight down the particle’s velocity and the vector it contributes is radial, so its transverse part, which is the only part a photon can carry, is exactly zero. Work the algebra out for one line on its own and the whole angular shape is elementary:

One line, alone: an exact zero along its velocity and a ring of maxima around it
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{P}_{\text{one}}(\theta)&=\frac{Q^{2}\beta^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{(1-\beta\cos\theta)^{2}}\,,\\[2pt] \frac{d\mathcal{P}_{\text{one}}}{d\cos\theta}&\propto\ \beta-\cos\theta\,,\\[2pt] \max\mathcal{P}_{\text{one}}&=\frac{Q^{2}\beta^{2}}{1-\beta^{2}}=Q^{2}\beta^{2}\gamma^{2} \quad\text{at }\ \cos\theta=\beta \end{aligned} \]
\(\theta\) is measured from \(\hat\beta\). The numerator vanishes at \(\theta=0\) and \(\theta=\pi\); the denominator makes the forward half far brighter than the backward half. The maximum therefore sits on a ring at \(\cos\theta=\beta\), that is at \(\theta=\arccos\beta\), which closes in on the velocity as \(\beta\to1\) — the beaming everyone expects — while the value on the ring grows only as \(\gamma^{2}\), not as \((1-\beta)^{-2}\). Numbers, for one unit charge: a line at \(\beta=0.5\) peaks at \(0.3333\) on a ring \(60^{\circ}\) wide; one at \(\beta=0.99\) peaks at \(49.25\) on a ring \(8.1^{\circ}\) wide. That is a factor of \(148\), not four orders of magnitude. And it stays finite: a massive line has \(1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta_k\ge1-\beta_k>0\). A genuine collinear pole needs a massless charged line, which the sliders below cannot reach and which this page does not draw. Use the “paint one line alone” control under the sphere to see the zero and the ring by themselves; with several lines the terms interfere and the zeros move.

The second feature is a condition, not a picture. The whole expression is only well defined when the total charge balances, \(\sum_k\eta_kQ_k=0\). Otherwise it is not invariant under the change of polarization that gauge invariance permits, and the pattern below is a picture of a quantity that no experiment measures. The panel says so when you unbalance it, prints the exact amount by which the soft factor moves, and withdraws the claim that what you are looking at is a scattering event rather than a plot of a formula.

Try two things. Put two particles on the same direction and give them the same speed, and their two rings merge into one: the soft factor depends on a line only through \(\eta_kQ_k\vec\beta_k/(1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta_k)\), so two lines with the same full velocity \(\vec\beta\) contribute exactly as one line of charge \(\eta_1Q_1+\eta_2Q_2\) does. Matching the directions alone is not enough — the speed enters the denominator too, and two lines that point the same way at different speeds have rings of different radii and do not merge. Then take a configuration you like and slide every speed down towards zero: the pattern keeps its shape and loses its contrast, because at small \(\beta\) each term becomes \(\eta_kQ_k\vec\beta_k\) and the sum degenerates to a single dipole. The rich structure is relativistic.

This is a picture of a function on the sphere of directions at infinity. The next two stations ask what that function is conserved against — what quantity it is the flux of, angle by angle.

Station 6 · memory, and why the soft theorem was inevitable

The same number, from a clock and from a spectrum

Put a second charge, light and free, a long way from the first one, and let it sit there. The shell from station 2 sweeps past it. While the shell is passing, the test charge feels a sideways force and starts to move. Then the shell is gone. The question that matters is not what happened during; it is what is left after.

The familiar anchor is an impulse. A force acting briefly on a free mass leaves it with a velocity equal to the time integral of the force divided by the mass, and nothing else about the force survives: \(\Delta\vec v=\frac{q_{\text{test}}}{m_{\text{test}}}\int\vec E_{\text{source}}\,dt\).

But that integral, taken over a real window at a real detector, is not one thing. The field there has a \(1/r^{2}\) Coulomb part as well as a \(1/r\) radiation part, and the Coulomb part contributes a drift that keeps growing as the window is opened. This station keeps the two apart throughout and never adds them up under one name: the second figure below is the Coulomb drift, on its own, in closed form. What the soft theorem controls — and the only quantity anything is equated to here — is the coefficient of the radiation part:

The quantity this station computes: the \(1/r\) radiation memory
\[ \int dt\;\vec E^{(1/r)}_{\text{rad}} \;=\;\frac{\vec{\mathcal N}(\hat n)}{r}, \qquad \vec{\mathcal N}(\hat n)=\int ds\; \frac{\hat n\times\bigl[(\hat n-\vec\beta)\times\dot{\vec\beta}\bigr]}{(1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta)^{2}} \]
Everything printed below is \(\vec{\mathcal N}\) itself, per unit source charge: the source charge and the test particle’s \(q_{\text{test}}/m_{\text{test}}\) are stripped out, so to get a velocity kick in ordinary units you multiply back by \(q\,q_{\text{test}}/m_{\text{test}}\) and divide by \(r\). This is the leading transverse radiation-order integral and nothing else; it is not the finite‑window integral of the full electric field, which also contains the drift.

The interesting claim is that this number is the same as the coefficient of the pole that station 3 found in the frequency domain. There is a reason it has to be, and it is one line:

Why the time integral and the zero-frequency component are the same object
\[ \tilde{\vec E}(\omega)=\int dt\;e^{\,i\omega t}\vec E^{(1/r)}_{\text{rad}}(t) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tilde{\vec E}(0)=\int dt\;\vec E^{(1/r)}_{\text{rad}}(t)=\frac{\vec{\mathcal N}(\hat n)}{r} \]
A permanent, direction-dependent value of that integral is a nonzero zero‑frequency component of the radiation field, and therefore a simple pole in the vector potential. In radiation gauge \(\vec E=-\partial_t\vec A\), so with the convention above an integration by parts gives \(\tilde{\vec E}=i\omega\tilde{\vec A}\), that is \(\tilde{\vec A}(\omega)=\tilde{\vec E}(\omega)/(i\omega)\), a simple pole at \(\omega=0\) with residue \(\vec{\mathcal N}/(i\,r)\) — the memory, carrying the \(i\) the convention puts there. A pole in the amplitude for emitting a photon is exactly what a soft theorem asserts. Reading this the other way round: a scattering theory whose late‑time fields carry memory cannot have a soft limit without a pole. This is the step that requires hypotheses — the field has to settle down for the integral to converge at all — and the sister lane’s station 9 breaks them on purpose to show what they were doing.

The rest of this station is the arithmetic, done three ways, with everything printed.

Now the part that is easy to overstate, and which this page will not. The quantity above is the radiation‑order term. It is not the whole of \(\int\vec E\,dt\) at a real detector over a real finite window, because the Coulomb part of the field also contributes to that integral, and for a charge that was sitting still before the kick, its contribution grows without bound as the window is opened backwards in time — a static field integrated forever.

Stand back from the numbers. A charge changed velocity, and a long way away a free test charge was left permanently moving, by an amount that depends only on the change and on the angle. Fourier transform that and you have a pole at zero frequency, with the pole’s residue fixed by the same data. Two subjects that grew up separately — the memory effect, from general relativity in the 1970s and 80s, and soft theorems, from quantum field theory in the 1960s — are here two ways of writing the same coefficient down. That is one edge of the triangle, and it is now earned rather than asserted.

What is missing is the third corner. A conserved quantity is lurking: \(\vec{\mathcal N}(\hat n)\) is a vector field on the sphere of directions, and vector fields on spheres that come out of conservation laws are usually the divergence of something. Station 7 finds out what.

Station 7 · one conservation law per angle

Gauss’s law, refined until there is one of it for every direction

The familiar anchor is Gauss’s law: the total charge inside a region is the flux of the electric field through a surface around it, and if charge is conserved that flux does not change. One number, conserved. This station is about the fact that there is a much finer statement hiding inside it — not one conserved number but one for every function on the sphere of directions — and that the soft factor is what makes the finer statement work.

Two ingredients. The first is the Coulombic field at large distance. For a charge in uniform motion, the leading \(1/r^{2}\) part of the radial electric field, held at fixed retarded time and taken out to infinity, is not constant over the sphere: it depends on the angle, and the dependence is the same one that has been in every formula on this page.

The Coulombic profile at large distance Quoted
\[ \lim_{r\to\infty}r^{2}E_{r}\;=\;C_{\beta}(\hat n)\;=\;\frac{1-\beta^{2}}{(1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta)^{2}} \]
This is the angular part of \(F_{rt}\big|_{\mathcal I^{+}}\) at soft_Arxiv_update.tex:541-550, where it appears with the notes’ charge normalisation \(e^{2}Q_k/4\pi\) out front; here everything is per unit charge, Gaussian, \(c=1\). At \(\beta=0\) it is the constant Coulomb field. At \(\beta\ne0\) it is not constant, and the notes make a point of what that means. Expanded in Legendre polynomials the profile has a nonzero \(\ell=1\) moment — and, generally, nonzero moments at every \(\ell\): at \(\beta=0.72\), for instance, the coefficients run \(1.00,\,1.64,\,1.37,\,0.92,\,0.56,\dots\) for \(\ell=0,1,2,3,4\), so the \(\ell=2\) piece is nearly as large as the \(\ell=1\) piece. The point the notes make is about the \(\ell=1\) moment in particular: it is not the electric dipole moment of the usual multipole expansion, since a single moving charge has no dipole moment, and the source is explicit that the two “are not to be confused” (soft_Arxiv_update.tex:553). Its total over the sphere is the charge, as it must be: \(\int C_{\beta}\,d\Omega=4\pi\).

The second ingredient is the constraint. Maxwell’s equations at future null infinity have a leading term in their \(1/r\) expansion, and it is a relation between how the Coulombic profile changes with retarded time and what the radiation is doing:

The leading constraint at future null infinity Quoted
\[ \partial_u F_{ru}^{(2)}+D^{z}F^{(0)}_{uz}+D^{\bar z}F^{(0)}_{u\bar z}+e^{2}j_u^{(2)}=0 \]
soft_Arxiv_update.tex:699-704, equation (abelianconstrainteq); \(D_z\) is the covariant derivative of the unit round sphere. The last term is the charge current piercing null infinity. For the kicked charge on this page it is zero, because the charge is massive: it ends up at timelike infinity, never crossing \(\mathcal I^{+}\), and its own field is what appears in the first term. If a massless charged particle crossed \(\mathcal I^{+}\), that term would have to come back.

Integrate the constraint over all retarded time. The first term telescopes to the difference between the profile long after the news arrives and long before it. The middle terms are the sphere divergence of the transverse radiation field integrated over all time — and that integral is the memory vector from station 6. So, per angle:

One conservation law per direction
\[ C_{\beta_f}(\hat n)-C_{\beta_i}(\hat n)\;=\;D_A\,\mathcal{M}^{A}(\hat n), \qquad \vec{\mathcal M}(\hat n)=\hat n\times\Bigl(\hat n\times\Bigl[\tfrac{\vec\beta_f}{1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta_f}-\tfrac{\vec\beta_i}{1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta_i}\Bigr]\Bigr) \]
In the reference core’s own words, which are the sentence this page is licensed to print: “at every angle on future null infinity, \(C_f-C_i=D_AM^A\): the change in the \(1/r^{2}\) Coulomb profile is the sphere divergence of the DC transverse radiation field, not that field itself; no null charge‑flux term appears here because the kicked source is massive.” The distinction matters and this page will not blur it: the difference of the Coulomb profiles is not the memory, it is the divergence of the memory. There is a one‑line reason the identity holds at all — \(\vec{\mathcal M}\) is the sphere gradient of \(\log(1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta)\) differenced between the two velocities, and \(D_AD^{A}\log(1-\hat n\cdot\vec\beta)=C_{\beta}-1\), with the constant cancelling in the difference.

What has been shown is a conservation law with an angle attached. Weight the identity by any function \(\varepsilon(\hat n)\) and integrate over the sphere and you get one conserved quantity per \(\varepsilon\): a hard piece, which is the charge weighted by where it went, and a soft piece, which is the memory weighted by the derivative of \(\varepsilon\). Constant \(\varepsilon\) gives back ordinary charge conservation and the soft piece integrates away. Every other \(\varepsilon\) gives something new. That infinity of conservation laws is the third corner of the triangle, and the soft factor is not an accessory to it — it is the term that makes it balance.

This page stops one step short of the corner itself. Turning these classical conservation laws into symmetry generators, and their Ward identities into the soft theorem, is the content of the lecture notes at soft_Arxiv_update.tex:1064 and is reported here rather than derived.

An interlude, because the angles do not match up the way you expect

There is a wrinkle in all of this that took a long time to be noticed. A conservation law relates something at \(\mathcal I^{-}\) to something at \(\mathcal I^{+}\), and to write it down you have to say which direction on the incoming sphere corresponds to which direction on the outgoing one. The answer is not the obvious one. The Liénard–Wiechert field of a charge in uniform motion does not have the same value at the two ends; it satisfies \(\lim r^{2}F_{ru}(\hat x)\big|_{\mathcal I^{+}_{-}}=\lim r^{2}F_{rv}(-\hat x)\big|_{\mathcal I^{-}_{+}}\) — soft_Arxiv_update.tex:568-573 — matching at antipodal points.

So: the matching condition is checked in every example anyone has looked at, it is forced if you want the scattering problem to be defined at all, and as far as the person who built this subject knows there is no first‑principles derivation of it. That is the honest state of the third corner, and it is not a footnote. Be exact about what depends on it: the ledger above, \(C_f-C_i=D_A\mathcal{M}^A\), is a local statement at \(\mathcal I^{+}\) — it follows from the constraint equation and this solution, and it needs no matching condition at all. What needs the antipodal identification is the step after it, the conservation law across null infinity that ties a charge defined at \(\mathcal I^{-}\) to one defined at \(\mathcal I^{+}\), because without an identification of the two spheres there is nothing to equate.

One last remark, on the name. “Asymptotic symmetry” sounds like an approximation, and the conservation laws in this station are not approximate: each of them is an exact statement about exact solutions, checked here to the precision of the arithmetic. The word records where the symmetry becomes easy to recognise, not how true it is.

“I kind of dislike this word asymptotic symmetries because it suggests that they’re somehow not exact that they’re only true in some asymptotic sense but asymptotic symmetries are symmetries just like any other Symmetry and the reason they’re called asymptotic symmetries is that they typically take a simple and recognizable form when you look at how they act on the asymptotic regions of space-time”

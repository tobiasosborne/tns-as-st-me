# Digest — Colloquium: The Infrared Triangle in Quantum Gravity

- **Title:** The infrared triangle in quantum gravity (CSTQ colloquium, Center for SpaceTime and the Quantum; delivered remotely to Marseille)
- **Speaker:** Andrew Strominger
- **Video id:** `6kH2pl_zvMA` — https://youtu.be/6kH2pl_zvMA
- **Duration:** 01:08:23
- **Source file:** `refs/strominger-videos/cstq-6kH2pl_zvMA.md` (auto-captions; secondary source, not L3 ground truth)
- **Written companion:** arXiv:1703.05448 throughout; the last third of the talk post-dates the notes entirely.

**Arc.** A late-period overview, given after the lectures had become a Princeton book. Roughly the first
forty minutes re-tell the triangle; the last twenty present the celestial-current work with Guevara,
Himwich and Pate and the $w_{1+\infty}$ algebra. For our purposes the retelling matters less than the
frame he now puts around it. He opens not with results but with **three things we cannot do** —
define gravitational scattering, list its symmetries, or say what the right boundary conditions are —
and returns to that admission repeatedly. He is explicit about what the programme is *not* (new
microscopic physics), about where established content stops and speculation begins, about an
experimental hope that did not pan out, and — most valuable of all — about the exact status of
celestial holography: a proven rewriting, not yet a duality, because only one side of it has an
independent definition.

## Insights

### 1. The opening move is an admission, not a result
He frames the entire subject as resting on three unsolved problems, the first being that we do not know
how to *define* gravitational scattering. Coming from someone reviewing his own programme, this is a
notable choice of emphasis.

> "we know far less about it even at the classical level than is commonly presumed. And I'd like to draw
> attention to three major unsolved problems for scattering in general relativity and asymptotically
> flat space times. The first one is how do we define it?"

### 2. The well-posed initial-value problem is *not* the scattering problem
The precise reason the mathematicians' results do not settle the question: evolving diffeomorphism-
invariant Cauchy data is well defined, but scattering involves infinitely many quantities that are not
diffeomorphism invariant and are nonetheless physical. This is the cleanest statement of the gap
anywhere in the three transcripts.

> "if you take an asymptotically flat complete space-like slice and you specify all the … strictly
> diffeomorphism invariant data on that slice. And then you evolve it using the Einstein equation. That
> problem is well defined. But that is not the same as the scattering problem"

`[6kH2pl_zvMA @ 00:02:12]` — tags: methodology, asymptotic-symmetries, open-problems, pitfalls

### 3. The boost intuition pump — his standing answer to "it's just a gauge transformation"
Deployed here in thirty seconds. Nobody thinks an electron at rest and a moving electron are the same
state, yet a boost is a diffeomorphism. The general-relativistic version of the same phenomenon is just
subtler. (The same objection consumed ten minutes of Lecture 10's Q&A; this is the compressed rebuttal.)

> "a boost is a diffeomorphism. But nobody would say electron at rest and electron moving that that's
> the same thing. But in general relativity, it becomes much more subtle."

`[6kH2pl_zvMA @ 00:03:12]` — tags: pitfalls, asymptotic-symmetries, methodology, large-gauge

### 4. What makes a configuration physically inequivalent: a measuring device exists
The operational criterion, stated with an unusually vivid example — two gravitons leaving the poles,
supertranslated so that one departs fifty years early and the other a hundred years late. Diffeomorphic,
and distinguishable by an instrument. For a campaign about what memory measurement *means*, this is the
relevant definition of physicality.

> "That's a diffeomorphism, but but it's a physically inequivalent situation. We could construct a
> measuring device which would uh distinguish between these two possibilities."

`[6kH2pl_zvMA @ 00:16:49]` — tags: supertranslations, memory, experiment, methodology

### 5. …stated once more as a principle
The generalisation, one beat later.

> "So, we can measure things that are not related by diffeomorphisms. That are … that are not
> diffeomorphisms invariant."

### 6. Why supertranslations exist at all, in one sentence
The root cause: there is no preferred sphere at infinity, so general relativity gives you no way to tie
together "the same time" at different angles. Everything else follows from that.

> "in a general asymptotically flat spacetime, there is no preferred sphere at infinity … general
> relativity doesn't allow you to tie together the structure at the different points of the sphere"

`[6kH2pl_zvMA @ 00:15:47]` (continues at `00:16:49`) — tags: supertranslations, asymptotic-symmetries, vacuum-degeneracy

### 7. We still do not know the right boundary conditions — nor even the principle that would fix them
The most candid open problem in the talk, and a meta-level one: it is not that the calculation is hard,
it is that no criterion is known for what would count as the right answer. He includes his own community
in "mostly people have gotten it wrong".

> "it's very tricky how you choose these boundary conditions. And um we still don't know exactly how to
> choose them. Mostly people have gotten it wrong and then it they corrected it, then had to be
> corrected again, and I don't think we're at the end of that process … there's even a question of what
> is the principle that would determine what the right boundary conditions are."

`[6kH2pl_zvMA @ 00:13:40]` — tags: open-problems, methodology, asymptotic-symmetries, pitfalls

### 8. The retrospective verdict on BMS's own choice, compressed to six words
Too strong because they killed the superrotations; too weak because they missed the antipodal structure
that makes a scattering symmetry possible.

> "the BMS boundary conditions were both too strong and too weak."

`[6kH2pl_zvMA @ 00:34:37]` — tags: history, asymptotic-symmetries, methodology

### 9. Historical: the community actively tried to get rid of BMS symmetry
Not merely that people doubted it — that there was a research effort aimed at killing it off with a
boundary condition at spatial infinity, and that all of those attempts had problems. He includes his own
discomfort in the account.

> "So, this bothered me and this bothered BMS, and it was widely held that these groups uh acted on
> Scri plus and Scri minus, but you couldn't relate them in any way … many efforts were made to kind of
> kill this BMS symmetry by some kind of boundary condition at spatial infinity, but all of those had
> problems."

`[6kH2pl_zvMA @ 00:17:52]` (continues at `00:18:53`) — tags: history, asymptotic-symmetries, methodology

### 10. What "a symmetry of scattering" actually means: Earth and Mars
The plainest possible operational gloss on why $\mathrm{BMS}^+ \times \mathrm{BMS}^-$ alone is not good
enough and an antipodal pairing is needed. A symmetry is a licence to predict the second experiment from
the first.

> "if we do the same experiment on … Earth and then we do it again on Mars, we should be able to predict
> the answer of the second one from the first one. Because translations are our symmetry."

`[6kH2pl_zvMA @ 00:18:53]` — tags: asymptotic-symmetries, ward-identities, methodology

### 11. Supertranslation charge conservation is energy conservation at every angle
The single most quotable compression in the talk, and a genuinely useful mental model — with the caveat
about the linear total-derivative term attached in the same sentence rather than buried.

> "you can think of this as energy conservation at every angle. It's not just that the total energy
> equals incoming energy equals the total outgoing energy. It's true that the energy coming in from the
> South Pole flux integrated over all advanced time is equal to the integral of the energy flux at the
> North Pole"

### 15. Self-criticism of the field: the experiments are done, the theorists are behind
Delivered mildly but unmistakably. The point is that no new apparatus and no new microscopic theory is
needed to answer the question of what the symmetries are — the obstacle is purely theoretical work not
yet done.

> "this is a basic question about nature which all the experiments have already been done that would
> help us answer this question. And it's a job for the theorists and we haven't done our job. Uh and
> we've had a long time to do it in."

### 16. The tower of symmetries is not merely unclassified — there is no proposal on the table
A stronger statement than "this remains to be worked out".

> "there's a whole infinite power [tower] of these symmetries, but they haven't been organized. We
> don't … know what they all are. And … there isn't even a [proposal] on the table for what they all
> are."

### 17. Formalism choice is a tractability question, not a correctness one
A methodological principle worth transplanting. Hamiltonian, covariant phase space, S-matrix,
Lagrangian, soft theorems, boundary-condition analysis — all equivalent, all valid, and the real work is
picking the one in which the problem is easiest.

> "you could use the Hamiltonian formalism. You could look for charges that commute with the
> Hamiltonian. You could use the covariant phase space formalism, the S matrix, Lagrangian … They should
> all work. But it's a hard problem and it's important to find the simplest approach."

### 18. …and the specific reason he now prefers celestial currents
Read against insight 7, this is the punchline: he does not know the right boundary conditions, so he
has moved to a formulation that does not require him to state any.

> "it side steps having to make a gauge choice or a statement about boundary conditions."

### 19. A deep-versus-bookkeeping judgment on his own recent work
Unusually direct about how much is new. The physical content is the asymptotic constraint equations of
general relativity; the contribution is a change of language that makes a different community's tools
apply.

> "you just use the asymptotic constraint equations of general relativity. That's all it is, but written
> in a very unfamiliar form … in a form which anybody who studied two-dimensional conformal field theory
> would say, 'Oh, yeah, I know that formula.'"

### 20. The discovery order was backwards from how it is usually told

> "we didn't set out to find W1+infinity. It's kind of the nicest infinite dimensional algebra. We found
> something which looked more complicated and then we found a transformation which turned it into this
> this W algebra."

### 23. The status of celestial holography: a proven rewriting is not a duality
The most important judgment in the talk, and the one least likely to be found in a paper abstract. That
any amplitude can be written as a celestial correlator obeying the symmetries is *proved*, not
conjectured. What is open is whether it is useful, and whether the right-hand side can be defined
intrinsically.

> "it's not a conjecture … It's been proven that you could rewrite until you're blue in the face any
> scattering amplitude in quantum gravity … so it looks like a correlation function of conformal field
> theory and it will obey the symmetries. That's been proven … The question is whether it's useful …
> and even more whether you could intrinsically define the right hand side on its own right"

`[6kH2pl_zvMA @ 01:03:27]` — tags: celestial, methodology, open-problems, pitfalls

### 24. …with the comparison to AdS/CFT that makes the deficiency concrete
Six words that state exactly what is missing.

> definitions and wherever we can compare them, they agree. Here we only have a left."

### 25. …and a limit on the ambition, for the real world specifically

> "we don't really expect to get the right for the real world because … in the string theory examples,
> we know everything all the way to the the infinite UV … but that's not the real world. We don't expect

### 28. Every current in the tower has an observable memory effect, computable by algorithm
A structural claim worth recording: memory is not a special feature of the leading charge but a generic
consequence of each conserved current, with a mechanical procedure for extracting it.

> "each of these each current has an observable memory effect. The first one um is the standard memory
> effect … And then there are going to be more effects with at at every order. And there's a algorithmic
> way to compute what these effects are."

`[6kH2pl_zvMA @ 00:51:49]` — tags: memory, charges, celestial, soft-theorems

### 29. The price of the boost-weight basis, stated rather than hidden
Trading momentum eigenstates for boost eigenstates buys conformal covariance and costs simplicity of
translations, which now shift boost weight rather than acting diagonally. He notes it as a cost.

> "the price you pay for doing this, here translations act in a simple way. Here, they act in a funny
> way. They transform an operator from one boost weight to an operator with a shifted boost weight."

`[6kH2pl_zvMA @ 00:42:07]` — tags: celestial, methodology

### 30. Historical judgment: the holographic principle begins with Bekenstein–Hawking
Offered in passing while answering a question, but it is a clear statement of where he thinks the idea
starts.

> "the first statement of the holographic principle is really the … Bekenstein uh Hawking area entropy
> formula, which says that the number of degrees of freedom of a black hole grows like the surface of
> the black hole, not the volume."

`[6kH2pl_zvMA @ 00:59:13]` — tags: history, black-holes, celestial

### 31. Three independent routes converged on superrotations
A narrative he gives only in the Q&A: de Boer and Solodukhin proposed the hyperbolic-slicing picture
"without really any evidence"; Barnich and Troessaert arrived at the same objects by loosening BMS's
boundary conditions to allow meromorphic singularities; then a soft theorem proved by ordinary Feynman
diagrams established the symmetry. Convergence from unrelated directions is his evidence that the
structure is real.

> "they they conjectured that without really any evidence … then uh Compère and I proved a soft theorem
> using traditional particle theory type Feynman diagram methods … showed that this symmetry group was
> there"

`[6kH2pl_zvMA @ 01:01:22]` — tags: history, superrotations, celestial, soft-theorems

### 32. The symmetry algebra looks over-constrained, and he finds that interesting rather than alarming
An honest reaction to his own result.

> "There's so many symmetries here in this celestial holography. You wouldn't even think there was a
> theory that could have all those symmetries. It seems over constrained. But we know because we can
> construct examples that … they do exist."

### 33. Retrospective on where the whole programme came from
Asked whether celestial holography extends back to AdS, he inverts the question: the flat-space
programme was itself an extension of what had been learned in AdS, not the other way round.

> "I would kind of phrase it the other way around that we suitably interpreted what … had been done in
> ADS and extended it to flat space. That's … how it started."

## Deltas vs the written lectures (arXiv:1703.05448) and the published papers

- **The talk opens on what is unknown, not on what was achieved.** The notes open with the triangle and a
  survey of new developments. Here the frame is three unsolved problems, the first of which is that we
  cannot define gravitational scattering (insights 1–2). That reordering is itself the retrospective.
- **Boundary conditions are declared an open meta-problem.** The notes say only that asymptotic flatness
  "can become very technical, and there are different ways of defining it". The talk says nobody knows
  the right conditions, that repeated corrections have been needed, that the process is not finished, and
  that there is not even a principle for deciding (insight 7). Nothing of this candour is in the written
  source.
- **"It's a job for the theorists and we haven't done our job."** The notes close their discussion of the
  soft-theorem tower with "a complete classification … has not appeared". The spoken version is a
  self-assessment of the field, delivered about his own programme (insights 15–16).
- **Status of celestial holography, stated with a precision the literature rarely matches.** The notes
  present flat-space holography as a central motivation and cite the celestial-CFT literature. The talk
  separates what is proved (any amplitude can be rewritten as a celestial correlator with the right
  symmetries) from what is open (usefulness; an intrinsic definition of the boundary theory) and gives
  the one-sided-duality diagnosis (insights 23–25). For a campaign that must not overclaim, this is the
  model of how to state such a thing.
- **Everything from `00:36:46` onward post-dates the notes.** Celestial currents, the boost-weight basis,
  the classified tower, the algebra and its reduction to $w_{1+\infty}$, and the observation that each
  current carries its own memory effect computable algorithmically (insight 28) have no counterpart in
  the written source — `W_{1+` and `Bakas` appear zero times in `soft_Arxiv_update.tex`.
- **A deep-versus-bookkeeping judgment on that new work.** He says the physics is the asymptotic
  constraint equations and the contribution is the repackaging (insight 19), and that the algebra was
  found by accident rather than design (insight 20). Papers do not usually say this.
- **Speculation is marked in-line.** Insight 21. The notes flag open problems but do not draw a line
  across the middle of the presentation.
- **Experimental status has moved, and one hope has died.** The notes say memory has "a decent chance of
  being measured in the coming decades" and cite proposals; the talk names LIGO and pulsar timing arrays
  as actively pursuing it (insight 27) and reports that the LHC spin-memory idea does not appear to work
  (insight 26). The notes mention the Einstein Telescope proposal for spin memory but no LHC scheme.
- **The Goldstone language is used here only as translation.** At `00:27:17` he says "the words in
  particle physics is that supertranslation symmetry is spontaneously broken" — presented as a
  vocabulary equivalence rather than a claim. Worth reading against the Lecture 9 guest lecturer's
  explicit scepticism about that dictionary, and against the notes, which have a subsection built on it.
- **Two intuition pumps not in the notes.** The North-Pole/South-Pole gravitons separated by decades
  under a supertranslation, with the insistence that a measuring device could tell them apart
  (insight 4); and the Earth-and-Mars gloss on what a scattering symmetry buys you (insight 10).
- **The sociological history is spoken only.** That the field tried to *kill* BMS symmetry with a
  spatial-infinity boundary condition and failed (insight 9), and the three-route convergence story for
  superrotations (insight 31). The notes cite the same papers without the narrative.
- **Reverse deltas (in the notes, absent here).** The colloquium drops the echoing-triangles catalogue
  (supersymmetry, double-soft, higher dimensions, cosmology), the infrared-divergence and
  Faddeev–Kulish discussion, colour memory, the $\mathcal{N}=4$ Yang–Mills motivation, and the entire
  soft-hair / information-paradox application — black holes appear here only through the
  Bekenstein–Hawking remark at `00:59:13`. Given how central soft hair is to the notes, its near-absence
  from a late overview is itself a datum about shifted emphasis.
